Drying guide & FAQ — UK laundry tips

PegCast (Peg Forecast) turns the UK weather forecast into one simple number: today's drying index. Instead of guessing whether the washing line will work, check the score, hang your laundry on the best days, and save money on tumble-dryer electricity. This free guide explains how the score works, when British weather suits outdoor drying, and practical tips to get clothes dry quickly, fresh and crease-free.

How the PegCast drying score works

Across the daylight drying window — from one hour after sunrise to one hour before sunset, so the window shifts with the seasons — PegCast blends seven live weather readings into a single 0–100 score. No single factor decides the result: a warm but humid, windless day can still dry slowly, while a cool, dry, breezy day can work surprisingly well.

Temperature (20%)Warmer air absorbs more moisture, so water evaporates from fabric faster. Around 15°C or above with sun is comfortable; below about 8°C outdoor drying becomes slow.
Humidity (25%)Damp, humid air can only take a little extra water. Below 60% humidity dries well; above 80% the washing may stay damp all day.
Wind (25%)Moving air sweeps moisture away from the fabric. A gentle breeze is ideal; still air lets a humid layer build around each item.
Dew point (15%)The temperature at which air becomes saturated. A high dew point means muggy, damp air that slows drying, even if the sun is out.
Sunlight — UV & cloud (15%)Direct sun warms the fabric and speeds evaporation. Bright, cloud-free days dry much faster than overcast ones.
Rain penaltyAny expected rain, drizzle, sleet or snow sharply lowers the score. The red banner warns when wet weather is likely in the next six hours.

How to read the bands: Green (75+) means hang outside with confidence; orange (35–74) means it will dry, but slowly — hang early and bring in before evening; red (below 35) means use a tumble dryer, radiator or heated airer instead.

Best time to dry clothes outside in the UK

The UK maritime climate means drying conditions change fast, and the best days rarely arrive back-to-back. A quick forecast check before you load the machine saves wasted washes and re-washing.

Seasons

  • Spring (March–May): mixed — sunny, breezy days dry well; cold nights and showers are common. Watch the score before you hang out.
  • Summer (June–August): the best drying season. Long daylight hours, warmer temperatures and occasional breezes; whites, towels and bedding benefit most.
  • Autumn (September–November): often damp and misty; mornings stay wet and evenings bring dew. Mostly orange or red scores.
  • Winter (December–February): cold air holds little moisture, so outdoor drying is slow and frost can stiffen fabric. Use indoor drying for most washes.

Time of day

  • Hang out once the morning dew has lifted — the Today tab shows the recommended time based on sunrise, drying speed and sunset.
  • Bring washing in before dusk — dew forms as temperatures fall and re-dampens dry clothes overnight.
  • Avoid washing on red-score days; use the Planner tab to pick the best of the next three days.

12 practical tips for faster line drying

1. Spin longerFinish the cycle on 1200–1400 rpm for an extra two minutes; the washing machine removes far more water than the air ever will.
2. Shake each itemShake and pull garments straight before pegging to reduce creases and open the fabric fibres.
3. Peg cleverlyPeg hems, waistbands and seams rather than shirt shoulders, to avoid visible peg marks.
4. Give spaceLeave gaps between items so air circulates on both sides; a crowded line dries slowly.
5. Turn darks inside outProtect colours from sun fading, especially reds, blacks and denim.
6. Sun for whites, shade for coloursHang towels, sheets and whites in direct sun; wool, silk and darks in light shade.
7. Raise the air flowUse a rotary line or freestanding airer away from walls, fences and hedges that block the breeze.
8. Check the score first75+ means go; 35–74 means hang early and expect a slow day; below 35 stay indoors.
9. Beat the dewBring everything in before evening; overnight dew and high humidity undo a dry day.
10. Secure heavy itemsBedding, dresses and long coats catch the wind; use extra pegs on blustery days.
11. Pre-treat stainsDeal with fresh marks before washing using the Stain tab; never tumble or line-dry a stained item, as heat sets the mark.
12. Clean the lineWipe the washing line or airer before use; a dirty line transfers marks to clean laundry.

Fabrics that dry best outside

  • Cotton towels, sheets and bedding: heavy and slow; choose a green, breezy day and give plenty of space.
  • T-shirts, shirts and synthetics: lightweight polyester and cotton blends dry quickly; hang synthetics promptly to avoid stale smells.
  • Wool, knitwear and delicates: dry flat in shade; hanging heavy wool stretches the shape.
  • Denim and jeans: slow and heavy; turn inside out and avoid strong sun to prevent fading.
  • Underwear and small items: fast drying; group on a small airer indoors on poor days.

When to dry indoors instead

  • Rain, drizzle, sleet or snow forecast (watch for the red banner).
  • Humidity around 80% or higher all day.
  • Frost or temperatures near or below freezing — fabric can freeze rather than dry.
  • High pollen days if you suffer from hay fever.
  • Storm-force gusts that can tear items or blow dirt onto them.

Indoor options: a heated airer, a radiator with a drying rack, a dehumidifier in a closed room, or a tumble dryer on a low heat setting.

Frequently asked questions

What does the PegCast drying score mean?

It is a 0–100 index for the daylight drying window — roughly one hour after sunrise to one hour before sunset. 75+ (green) is great for outdoor drying; 35–74 (orange) means slow but possible; below 35 (red) means use a tumble dryer, radiator or heated airer.

What time of day should I hang washing outside?

Hang out after the morning dew has gone — the Today tab calculates the best time from sunrise, drying speed and sunset, so it shifts with the seasons. Bring everything in before late afternoon; evening dew and falling humidity can re-dampen dry clothes overnight.

Can I dry clothes outside in a UK winter?

Only on dry, calm, frost-free days, and even then drying is slow. Most winter washes are better on a heated airer, radiator or tumble dryer; check the score before risking the line.

Does wind help clothes dry?

Yes — a gentle breeze replaces the damp air around the fabric and speeds evaporation. Very strong gusts can damage items or cause peg marks, so secure bedding and long garments.

Why does humidity matter for drying?

Humid air holds little extra moisture, so water leaves the fabric slowly. Low humidity (under about 60%) dries well; high humidity (over 80%) usually means a slow, damp day.

Should washing dry in sun or shade?

Whites, towels and bedding benefit from direct sun, which also brightens fabric. Dark colours, reds, denim and wool should dry in shade to prevent fading and stretching.

How long does washing take to dry outside?

On a warm, dry, breezy day (75+) most items dry in about 2–4 hours. On an orange day it may take all day, and on a red day it will not dry outside at all. The Today tab shows an estimated drying time.

Is line drying cheaper than a tumble dryer?

Yes. A tumble dryer cycle typically costs well over £1 in electricity, while outdoor drying uses none. On poor days a heated airer or dehumidifier usually costs less than a full tumble cycle.
